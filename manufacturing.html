Grove & gin

Two operations, and that is the whole company

We contract and support the groves, and we run the gin they deliver to. There is no spinning mill, no loom shed and no cut-and-sew floor behind this — the fibre leaves us as baled lint and goes to somebody who knows what to do with it.

Scale

What one season looks like

Picking runs from late November into April. Ginning follows the picking by a few days and finishes in May; the rest of the year is grove work, warehouse despatch and next season's contracting.

The grove

How the crop is grown

The groves are not ours; they belong to the families who farm them. What we do is contract the crop before the season, pay a published premium, and provide the agronomy support the organic certificate depends on.

Contracting

The crop is contracted before the season at a published price. The farms hold organic certification, inspected annually, and we buy the whole contracted crop rather than cherry-picking the good lots.

Perennial management

Instead of tillage and sowing, the annual cycle is pruning, mulching and canopy management. Soil is not turned over each season, so root systems and soil biology persist between harvests.

Inputs and intercrops

Rain-fed, with farmyard manure and botanical sprays only. Wide spacing leaves usable ground between trees, so pulses and millets are grown in the alleys — which spreads the family's risk across more than one harvest.

Picking

Bolls open unevenly, so trees are picked by hand over three to five passes. Pickers use cotton sacks, not synthetic ones — the single biggest source of contamination in Indian lint is the bag it was carried in.

Collection

Seed cotton is collected and moved to the gin the same week. Weighing happens on our bridge in front of the grower, and payment follows within 14 days, direct to a bank account.

Renewal

Productivity is kept up by pruning back and eventually replacing individual trees — grove by grove, not field by field. The certified area never goes through a bare-soil phase.

The gin

Why roller, not saw

A saw gin pulls the lint off the seed with toothed blades. At 25–32 mm upland staple that is fine and fast. At 15–20 mm it is destructive: the staple shortens further, neps rise, and what arrives at a spinner's blowroom is measurably worse than what left the field.

Double-roller gins work the fibre off more gently and more slowly. They are the traditional machine for desi cotton and they are the reason the HVI numbers on the bale hold up against the ones on the offer.

Cleaned down between lots. The line is emptied and swept between lots. It costs us throughput, and it is the only way the figures on the offer match what is actually in the bale.

Intake

Weighed and moisture-checked as it arrives. Lots are stacked separately, never tipped into a common heap.

Drying and cleaning

Yard drying to bring moisture into range, then a single cleaning point. Hand-picked cotton arrives clean, so we take out less and keep more fibre length.

Roller ginning

Two shifts through the season. Outturn is recorded per lot, which is the first number our certifier reconciles.

Pressing

Pressed into 170 kg bales and numbered against the lot, so the HVI report that ships with them belongs to that cotton and no other.

Testing

Measured before it is sold

Every lot goes through our own HVI line before it is offered, so what you are quoted is a measurement rather than a description. The report travels with the bales.

For anything that needs third-party evidence — GM screening, species identification, residues — we use accredited external laboratories and pass the report through unedited, including the results we would rather not have had.

Certifications

What we hold, and what it covers

Certificates are issued to us by independent bodies — we are a grower and a ginner, not a certifier. Current scope certificates are available on request before you buy, and a transaction certificate ships with every order.

GOTS

Global Organic Textile Standard, scope covering ginning, baling and trade of certified organic lint.

OCS 100

Organic Content Standard for the lint we sell, with transaction certificates issued per shipment.

NPOP / NOP organic

Farm-level organic certification on the contracted acreage, inspected annually by an accredited certifier.

Fairtrade cotton

Held on roughly 40% of our contracted acreage.

Social compliance

SEDEX SMETA four-pillar audit at the gin, plus our own quarterly labour checks in the groves during picking.

What we do not hold

No OEKO-TEX or product-safety marks — those belong on finished textiles, and we do not make any. Do not let anyone tell you a bale of lint carries one.

A note on claims. Organic and Fairtrade claims belong to the certificate covering a specific lot, not to the company in general. Ask us for the transaction certificate for your order — if we cannot produce one, do not make the claim.

People and environment

How the operation is run

Growers

640 families under contract. We publish the premium paid over the state minimum support price each season, and payment is made within 14 days of intake, direct to bank accounts, with no commission agent in between.

Workforce

96 permanent staff and about 240 seasonal workers at the gin through the ginning months, 54% women, all on written contracts with statutory benefits and a canteen on site.

Water

The groves are rain-fed — no irrigation is drawn for cotton on our contracted acreage. Ginning is a dry process, so the gin's own water use is domestic rather than industrial.

Energy and waste

A 340 kW rooftop solar array covers about 40% of gin demand through the season. Gin trash and cotton stalk are composted and returned to the farms.

Come and see the gin running

December to March the line is going and the groves are still in picking. Two weeks' notice and you can see the crop, the gin line and the press in a single day.
