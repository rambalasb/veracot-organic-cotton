The Fibre

Tree cotton: Gossypium arboreum

A woody perennial cotton, domesticated on the Indian subcontinent and grown there for millennia. It stands as a shrub-tree well above waist height, bears bolls for several seasons from the same root system, and is picked by hand rather than stripped by machine. It is the only thing we grow and the only thing we sell — this page is the honest account of what it does well and where it does not belong.

Botany

Two different plants, one common name

"Cotton" covers several species in the genus Gossypium. The commodity trade is dominated by G. hirsutum — American upland cotton — an annual bush bred for long, fine, machine-friendly fibre. Tree cotton, G. arboreum, is an older, diploid Old World species: shorter in staple, coarser, tougher, and perennial in habit.

Locally it is the desi cotton: the fibre that clothed the subcontinent long before upland varieties arrived, and the one still spun by hand for khadi. Because it was displaced by higher-yielding hybrids rather than out-competed on quality, its strengths were never really superseded — they were just set aside.

Not kapok. Tree cotton is true cotton — seed-hair fibre from a Gossypium boll, ginnable and spinnable in the normal way. It is unrelated to kapok or silk-cotton from Ceiba and Bombax trees, whose hollow fibres are used as filling rather than spun.

Comparison

Tree cotton against upland cotton

Indicative typical values. Real figures vary by variety, region and season — treat this as orientation, not a specification.

Tree cotton · G. arboreum Upland cotton · G. hirsutum
Habit Woody perennial, grown as a standing tree or large shrub Annual bush, resown each season
Field life Several seasons from the same plant One season, then ploughed in
Genome Diploid Old World species Tetraploid New World species
Staple length Short — roughly 15–25 mm Medium to long — roughly 25–32 mm
Fineness Coarser fibre, higher micronaire Finer fibre, lower micronaire
Absorbency High — the traditional surgical-cotton fibre Good, but rarely specified for absorbency alone
Ginning Roller gin, which is gentler on short staple Saw gin, suited to longer staple at volume
Harvest Hand-picked in several passes as bolls open Often machine-picked or stripped in one pass
Water Usually rain-fed, on marginal or light soils Frequently irrigated
GM varieties None in commercial cultivation Widely planted as insect-resistant GM hybrids
Lint yield Lower per hectare Higher per hectare

The honest summary: tree cotton trades yield and staple length for hardiness, absorbency and a much simpler organic story.

Cultivation

How a grove is grown

Establishment

Trees are raised from seed at wide spacing and allowed to develop a woody frame. The first commercial picking comes later than a row crop's — the trade-off for not resowing every year.

Perennial management

Instead of tillage and sowing, the annual cycle is pruning, mulching and canopy management. Soil is not turned over each season, so root systems, organic matter and soil biology persist between harvests.

Intercropping

Wide spacing leaves usable ground between trees. Pulses, millets and other food crops are commonly grown in the alleys, which spreads the grower's risk across more than one harvest.

Pest and water strategy

The species is traditionally valued for tolerance to drought and to sucking pests, which reduces the pressure to spray — a material advantage when the fibre has to satisfy an organic standard.

Harvest

Bolls open unevenly, so trees are picked by hand over several passes. Selective picking means less trash, leaf and immature fibre reaching the gin than a single mechanical pass would deliver.

Renewal

Productivity is maintained by pruning back and, eventually, replacing individual trees — grove by grove, not field by field. The certified area does not go through a bare-soil phase.

Specifying it

Design to the fibre, not around it

Short staple and coarse micronaire set real limits. Fine, high-twist, high-count yarns are not where this fibre performs, and specifying it as if it were combed long-staple upland cotton produces a disappointing fabric and an avoidable argument with the mill.

  • Coarser counts — the fibre spins comfortably into medium and coarse yarns with visible character.
  • Absorbent constructions — towelling, wadding, muslin, surgical and hygiene articles play directly to its strength.
  • Texture as the point — slub, khadi handloom, denim weft and rustic wovens use the irregularity rather than fighting it.
  • Blending for hand — a 30/70 or 50/50 blend with organic upland cotton is the usual route to softness, and most of our spinning customers do exactly that.
  • Expect batch variation — a hand-picked perennial crop is less uniform than a machine-harvested annual one. Build tolerance into the spec.

End uses that suit it

  • Surgical cotton, wadding and hygiene articles
  • Towelling and bath textiles
  • Muslin, voile and baby wraps
  • Khadi and handloom apparel
  • Denim weft and workwear
  • Dhurries, upholstery and furnishing wovens

Non-GM by nature

Why the GM screen matters more here, not less

Genetically modified cotton in commercial cultivation is upland cotton. There are no commercially released GM varieties of tree cotton — so on paper, a GM screen on a tree cotton article should always pass.

That is exactly what makes it useful. A positive GM result on our lint would not usually mean somebody planted modified tree cotton; it would mean upland cotton got into the crop. The screen is therefore a test of species identity, not merely of seed choice — which is why we run it every season and send you the report.
